What Is a Hackathon? A Guide to Joining One from Kenya

A weekend, a team and a deadline. How hackathons work, who takes part, where to find them, and what you can get out of one even if you do not win.

The Tech Hut · Sunday, 22 November 2026

A hackathon is a short, intense event, usually one to three days or a few weeks online, where people form small teams and build a working project around a theme, then present it to judges. You do not need to be an expert, and many hackathons are free and welcome beginners. For someone building a tech career in Kenya, a hackathon is one of the fastest ways to get a real project into your portfolio, meet people already working in the industry, and sometimes win prize money or a job interview.

This guide explains how hackathons work, who takes part, how to join one from Kenya, where to find them, and how to get the most out of the weekend even if your team does not win.

What is a hackathon?

The word combines “hack”, in the old sense of building something clever quickly, and “marathon”. An organiser sets a challenge or a theme, such as education, health, fintech or AI agents, and a deadline. Teams then plan, build and present a prototype: a working app, a website, a dataset, a design or a hardware gadget.

Most hackathons share the same shape:

  1. Registration: you sign up alone or as a team, often on Devpost, Devfolio, lablab or the organiser's own page.
  2. Kick-off: the organisers explain the challenge, the rules, the judging criteria and the prizes, and sponsors introduce any tools or APIs you can use.
  3. Team formation: people without a team find one, often through a Discord server or an in-person mixer.
  4. Building: the bulk of the time. Mentors are usually on hand to help when you get stuck.
  5. Submission: a demo video, a link to your code, and a short write-up, before a hard deadline.
  6. Judging and demos: finalists present to a panel, and winners are announced.

A hackathon does not reward the most code. It rewards the clearest working answer to a real problem.

Online or in person

Online hackathons run on platforms such as Devpost and last anywhere from a weekend to several weeks, so you can take part from Nairobi, Eldoret or anywhere with an internet connection. In-person hackathons happen in one venue over a day or two, usually with food, Wi-Fi and mentors in the room. In Kenya, companies such as Africa's Talking regularly run in-person hackathons in Nairobi and Mombasa, and universities and tech communities host their own.

Who takes part?

Far more than developers. A strong team usually mixes skills:

  • Developers: front-end, back-end, mobile and data people who build the working product.
  • Designers: UI/UX designers who make the product usable and the demo look finished.
  • Product and business people: people who understand the problem, the users and how the idea would make money, and who often give the pitch.
  • Subject experts: a nurse at a health hackathon, a teacher at an edtech one, a farmer's child at an agritech one. They know which problems are real.
  • Students and beginners: many hackathons are labelled beginner friendly and some have separate prizes for first-timers.

Women in tech hackathons are common too. When we checked our hackathons list on 22 November 2026, one of the three in-person events in Kenya that week was Africa's Talking's Women in Tech hackathon on edtech in Nairobi.

How to participate, step by step

  1. Pick one that fits your skills and time. Read the theme, the dates, the eligibility rules and whether it is online or in person. A two-day event is easier to commit to than a month-long one.
  2. Register early. Popular in-person events fill up, and some online ones close registration before the submission deadline.
  3. Join or form a team of three to five. Aim for a mix: someone who builds, someone who designs, someone who can pitch. If you go alone, use the event's Discord or the team-matching session.
  4. Read the judging criteria before you write any code. Most judges score the problem, how well the solution works, originality, design and the presentation. Build for those.
  5. Scope small. Pick one problem for one type of user and make one main feature work end to end. A small thing that works beats a big thing that crashes in the demo.
  6. Use what the sponsors offer. Free credits, APIs and mentors are there to help, and many hackathons have extra prizes for using a sponsor's tool well.
  7. Leave time for the submission. Record a short demo video, write a clear README and check the links work, at least a few hours before the deadline.
  8. Practise the pitch. Two to three minutes: the problem, who has it, your solution, a live demo, and what you would build next.

Spend the first hour on the problem and the last hour on the demo. Most teams do the opposite.

What to bring to an in-person hackathon

Your laptop and charger, an extension cable if you can, mobile data as a backup for the venue Wi-Fi, headphones, a water bottle and something warm for late nights. Install your tools and test your development setup the night before, so the first hours go on building rather than downloading.

How you can benefit

A real project for your portfolio

Employers hiring juniors want proof you can build. A hackathon project comes with a deadline, a team, a problem statement and a demo video, which makes it far more convincing than a tutorial clone. Put it on GitHub with a clear README and add it to your CV under Projects. Our guide on how to write a CV shows how to describe it as a result.

Skills you cannot get from a course

Working under time pressure, splitting work in a team, cutting scope, and explaining technical work to non-technical judges are exactly the skills interviews test. A weekend hackathon practises all of them.

People who can hire or refer you

Mentors, judges and sponsors are often engineers and managers at companies that hire. Talk to them, ask for feedback, and connect on LinkedIn afterwards with a note about what you built. Many first jobs in tech come from a referral, and a hackathon is a room full of potential referrers.

Prizes, credits and programmes

Prizes range from cash and gadgets to cloud credits, internships and places in accelerator programmes. Some winning teams keep going: in October, tech-ish profiled Naulisako, a GirlCode Kenya hackathon winner building fare tracking for matatu owners.

A few cautions

  • Read the rules on ownership. Most hackathons let you keep your code, but some sponsor challenges claim rights to submissions. Check before you build something you plan to turn into a business.
  • Never pay to enter an unknown hackathon. The vast majority are free. Be wary of fees, unclear organisers or prizes that sound too good.
  • Look after yourself. Staying up all night rarely improves the project. Sleep a little, eat properly and keep the last hours for the demo.

Where to start

Pick one beginner-friendly hackathon in the next month, ideally online so the only cost is your time. Find two or three teammates, keep the idea small, and finish with a working demo. Whatever the result, you will leave with a project, new contacts and a story for your next interview.
