How to Write a CV in Kenya: 5 Steps, a Sample and ATS Tips

A recruiter scans your CV in seconds, and software often reads it first. Five steps to one that gets through both, with a full sample you can copy.

The Tech Hut · Monday, 16 November 2026

To write a CV for a job application, choose a clean one-column format, put your contact details and a three-line profile at the top, and list your experience and education in reverse order with bullets that show results. Then rewrite the CV for each job using the words in the advert, keep it to two pages, and save it with your name in the file name. That is how to write a CV that both a recruiter and a screening system can read.

Most CVs fail for ordinary reasons. They run to three pages of duties, they use a designed template that software cannot read, or the same file goes to fifty employers. A recruiter gives each CV a quick scan, and for many roles in Kenya, software sorts the applications before a person looks at any of them. If you have little experience, it is easy to assume you have nothing to show. You usually have more than you think, but you have to present it well.

The Tech Hut helps Kenyans build careers in tech, and the CV is where most applications are won or lost. This guide walks you through five steps, from layout to sending. It includes a full sample CV, copy-ready formulas for the profile and bullets, a fix list for common mistakes, and notes on getting past applicant tracking systems.

What a CV is and what to prepare first

What your CV has to do

A CV is a one to two page summary of your education, work experience and skills, and its only job is to get you an interview. Most employers you apply to in Kenya simply say “CV” for the document you send with an application. “Resume” is the US word for roughly the same thing. Academic CVs are the exception and run longer.

Recruiters read fast. They look for a job title, a recent employer, a relevant skill and a result. If those four things are easy to find, you stay in the pile. Writing a good CV is mostly about making that scan easy.

A CV is not a record of everything you have done. It is proof that you can do this one job.

Gather your material before you open a document

Writing from memory produces vague bullets. Spend 30 minutes collecting the raw material first:

  • The job advert you are targeting, saved as a PDF or screenshot, because adverts get taken down.
  • Every job, internship, volunteering role, apprenticeship, freelance gig and personal project, with start and end months.
  • Numbers: users, customers, sales, hours saved, tickets closed, team size.
  • Qualifications and certificates, with exact names and dates.
  • Links to LinkedIn, GitHub or a portfolio.
  • Two people who have agreed to act as referees.

Keep all of this in a master document with no length limit. Every CV you send is then a trimmed copy of the master, built for one role. That also makes the next application a 20-minute job instead of a fresh start.

Step 1. Choose a format and set up the page

Pick the CV type that fits your situation

Use a reverse chronological CV unless you have a specific reason not to. It lists your newest experience first, and it is what recruiters and software expect.

CV typeBest forHow it differs
Reverse chronologicalMost applicants with some work historyExperience first, newest to oldest
Skills-basedCareer changers, people with gaps or little formal workA skills section with proof leads, history is brief
AcademicResearch, teaching, university postsLonger, adds publications, grants and presentations

If you are a graduate with no job history, still use the chronological layout. Put projects, volunteering and apprenticeships where the jobs would go. That keeps the structure familiar to the reader.

Set up the layout

Good layout is invisible. The reader should never think about it. Use these settings:

  • Length: two A4 pages at most. One page is fine for students and first jobs. Go to three only for senior roles or long careers.
  • Font: Arial, Calibri or Times New Roman at size 11. Use one font throughout.
  • Headings: bold, size 14, in plain words such as Profile, Experience, Education, Skills.
  • Margins: about 2.5 cm on every side. Do not shrink them to squeeze in more.
  • Structure: one column, bullet points, no photo, no logos, no graphics.
  • Title: use your name at the top. Do not write “Curriculum Vitae”.

The sections go in this order:

SectionWhat goes in itSpace
HeaderName, phone, email, city, profile links2 lines
ProfileWho you are, one proof, what you want3 lines
Experience or projectsRole, employer, dates, 3 to 5 bullets eachMost of the page
EducationQualification, institution, dates2 to 4 lines
SkillsSkills the job asks for1 to 3 lines
ExtrasCertifications, awards, relevant volunteeringOptional

If you have more than two years of relevant work, put Experience before Education. If you are a recent graduate, do the reverse.

Plain, consistent formatting beats a clever template every time, because both humans and software read it without effort.

Step 2. Write the header and personal statement

Build a header that is easy to act on

The header exists so an employer can reach you in ten seconds. Include your full name, phone number, professional email address and city. Add a LinkedIn profile, GitHub account or portfolio link if it supports the job. For a developer or designer, the portfolio link is often more persuasive than anything else on the page.

Leave out your date of birth, age, marital status, ID number and photo unless the advert specifically asks. They add nothing to your case and take space from things that do. Skip your full street address as well. “Nairobi, Kenya” is enough, and it protects your privacy if the CV is shared or posted online. If your email address is a nickname or has a year of birth in it, create a new one in the form firstname.lastname.

Write a profile in three lines

A profile is two or three sentences at the top that tell the reader why to keep reading. It should name your level and field, give one proof point, and state the role you want. Skip phrases such as “hardworking team player”, which every applicant uses and no recruiter believes.

Copy this formula and fill in the brackets:

[Job title or level] with [time or type of experience] in [field]. [One result with a number]. Looking for a [target role] at a [type of employer] where I can [contribution].

Here is how it reads when filled in:

Junior data analyst with one year of internship experience in retail reporting. Built weekly sales dashboards in Power BI that cut manual reporting time from 6 hours to 1. Looking for a data analyst role at a fintech or retail company.

Write the profile last, after the rest of the CV is done. You will know your strongest points by then. Rewrite it for every application, because it is the part a recruiter reads first.

If a recruiter read only your profile, they should still know what you do and why you are worth a call.

Step 3. Add experience, education and skills

Turn duties into achievement bullets

Each bullet should show what you did and what changed because of it. A reliable formula is action verb, what you did, result. Start with verbs such as built, cut, led, analysed, designed or launched. Add a number wherever you honestly can.

Compare these two bullets:

  • Weak: “Responsible for sales reports.”
  • Strong: “Built a weekly sales report in Excel for 12 branches, cutting preparation time from 6 hours to 1.”

Use three to five bullets for your latest role and fewer for older ones. Summarise anything older than ten years in a line. If a bullet does not help you win this specific job, delete it.

If you have little formal experience, use what you have. Projects, volunteering, community work, freelance jobs and apprenticeships all count. Describe them exactly as you would a job, with a title, dates and results. If you have a gap, state the reason briefly with dates and list what you did during it, such as a course, caring responsibilities or freelance work. Employers see gaps often, and a short honest line works better than silence.

Handle education, skills and extras

List education with the qualification, institution and dates, newest first. Add modules or a final project only if they relate to the job. For skills, copy the exact names of the tools and methods the advert asks for, but only those you can discuss in an interview. Be ready to prove every line.

Interests belong on the CV only when they support the role, such as running a tech meetup for a community manager. Referees are optional. Some employers ask for them on the application, in which case list two people who have agreed, with their roles and contacts. Otherwise “References available on request” is enough, and you can drop it if space is tight.

A complete sample CV

This sample is for a fictional junior data analyst. Copy the structure and replace the content with your own.

AMINA OTIENO

Nairobi, Kenya | +254 7XX XXX XXX | amina.otieno@email.com | linkedin.com/in/aminaotieno | github.com/aminaotieno

PROFILE

Junior data analyst with one year of internship experience in retail reporting. Built weekly sales dashboards in Power BI that cut manual reporting time from 6 hours to 1. Looking for a data analyst role at a fintech or retail company.

EXPERIENCE

Data Analyst Intern | Kijani Retail Ltd, Nairobi | Jan 2025 to Dec 2025

  • Built 4 Power BI dashboards used by store managers in 12 branches
  • Cleaned and joined sales data from 3 sources using SQL and Excel, cutting weekly reporting from 6 hours to 1
  • Presented monthly findings to the operations director, leading to a change in restocking days

PROJECTS

M-Pesa Spending Tracker | Python, pandas | github.com/aminaotieno/mpesa-tracker

  • Analysed 2,000 anonymised transactions and charted spending by category

EDUCATION

BSc Statistics | University of Nairobi | 2020 to 2024

SKILLS

SQL, Excel (pivot tables, Power Query), Power BI, Python (pandas), data cleaning, stakeholder reporting

CERTIFICATIONS

Power BI course certificate | 2025

Every bullet should answer one question for the reader: what changed because you were there?

Step 4. Tailor the CV and get it past the software

Match your CV to the advert

A tailored CV is the same facts, reordered and reworded for one job. Read the advert and highlight the five to eight requirements that appear most clearly, usually in the skills and qualifications sections. Then check that your CV shows each of them, using the same words where they are true for you.

For example, if the advert asks for “Power BI dashboards” and your CV says “visual reports”, change it to “Power BI dashboards”. Move the bullet that proves it to the top of that role. Then adjust your profile so its second sentence matches the role's main need. This takes 15 to 20 minutes per application, and it is the single biggest change you can make to your response rate.

Do not claim skills you lack. Keywords you cannot back up get you an interview you will fail, and your references and certificates can be checked.

Make the file readable by applicant tracking systems

An applicant tracking system, or ATS, is software that reads your CV into plain text, sorts it against the advert and ranks candidates. Many employers publish roles through systems such as Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Pinpoint and SmartRecruiters, and The Tech Hut's jobs board reads openings from those hiring systems. Most parsers fail on the same things:

  • Tables, text boxes and multiple columns, which scramble the reading order.
  • Icons, logos, skill-rating bars and images, which are skipped or turned into junk characters.
  • Unusual headings like “My journey”, which the system cannot map to Experience.
  • Contact details placed in the page header or footer, where some parsers miss them.

Use standard headings, plain text and full names for qualifications, written as the certificate gives them. Follow the advert on file type. If it does not say, a .docx file is the safer choice for a portal, and a PDF is fine when you email a person directly.

If software cannot read your CV, a person never will, so check the plain-text version before you send.

Step 5. Proofread, save and send

Proofread like a stranger

One typo in a CV tells an employer you will miss details on the job. Wait a few hours after finishing, then check it in three ways. Change the font and size temporarily so your brain reads it as new text. Read it aloud, then read it from bottom to top, one line at a time. Finally, check every phone digit and email character, since a wrong digit loses you the call.

Ask someone else to read it too. Tell them to look for anything unclear, not only mistakes. Also confirm that dates are consistent, that bullets all use the same punctuation, and that employer names are formatted the same way everywhere.

Save it properly and send it with a note

Name the file Firstname-Lastname-CV, not “Document1” or “CV final 3”. A recruiter with 80 downloads needs to find yours. Keep the same file name format for every employer, and change the content, not the title.

Send a short cover note unless the employer says not to. Three or four sentences in the email body work: the role you are applying for, one reason you fit, and a line that the CV is attached. If you use AI tools, use them to summarise the advert, suggest wording or proofread. Do not let them write the whole CV or invent details. Generic, robotic CVs are easy for recruiters to spot, and every claim must still be yours.

Send a CV you can defend line by line in an interview.

Mistakes that sink a CV

MistakeFix
Three or more pagesCut to two. Delete old and irrelevant roles
Lists of dutiesRewrite each bullet with a result
One CV sent to every employerTailor the profile and top bullets per role
Graphics, columns and iconsMove to a plain single-column layout

Exaggerated skills or gradesRemove them. Employers check

Your next move

Writing a CV comes down to five steps: pick a plain format, write a sharp header and profile, turn your experience into results, tailor it to the advert, then proofread and send it under a clean file name. Build your master document once, and every future application becomes a quick edit instead of a rewrite.
