Featured · Health tech

Murang'a says the national government switched off its hospital records system; the Health Ministry says it never controlled it

Governor Irungu Kang'ata went public on Wednesday after Murang'a County's AfyaKE system stopped working on Tuesday 13 October. AfyaKE runs the county's public hospitals: patient registration, medical records, laboratory, pharmacy, patient tracking and referrals, plus billing and telemedicine. Kang'ata says the national government "switched off this system and forced Murang'a to join the SHA New System", and that Health CS Aden Duale promised in his presence that AfyaKE would be linked to the Social Health Authority rather than shut down. The Ministry of Health says it does not own, control or manage AfyaKE and does not hold its source code, calls it a private arrangement between the county and its developers, denies that the Digital Health Agency refused certification (it says neither the county nor the developers ever applied), and promises that SHA patients in Murang'a will keep receiving care. The county is moving its facilities to the national Taifa Care platform, expects two to three days of disruption and hopes to be back to normal by Monday 19 October. It says AfyaKE was running in 165 facilities, against 151 in a 2024 Council of Governors case study, and that patient data built up since 2023 is now out of reach; Techweez notes that describes lost access, not deletion. Eastleigh Voice reported that some Level 2 and Level 3 facilities have stopped services and cannot claim SHA refunds, but no list of affected facilities has been published. Two caveats matter. No server log, hosting record or written notice has been published showing who disabled the system, so neither the county's accusation nor the ministry's denial is confirmed, and the governor's claim that annual health revenue rose from KES 80 million to KES 800 million has no audited accounts behind it. On the rules: under the 2025 data exchange regulations the Digital Health Agency cannot connect a system without certification, and under the 2025 health information regulations a controller whose data access is discontinued must hand over a copy, inform the people affected and notify the Agency within seven days. Whether that has happened in Murang'a is not known.

SPACE

Kenyan-founded Satlyt raises US$8 million to run AI on satellites, but the NASA headline is mostly not cash

Satlyt writes software that runs data processing on a satellite's own computer, so the spacecraft sends back a result instead of raw files. The seed round, announced on 1 October, is US$8 million (about KES 1.04 billion), led by non sibi ventures with TLCOM, Antler, Launch Africa Ventures, Enza Capital, Slauson & Co. and Axian Investment; no valuation was given. Founder Rama Afullo is a Kenyan-American engineer who worked in Google's cloud business and briefly on Starlink at SpaceX in 2024. Satlyt owns no satellites and Tech-ish names no paying customers. Its first two in-orbit missions ran in March: a Gemma-based troubleshooting tool on a Momentus spacecraft sent a 469-byte diagnosis in place of 1,319 bytes of raw logs, about 64% less. The company has a Nairobi headquarters, a Kenyan subsidiary and a large part of its engineering team there, with a US base in Sunnyvale, and signed memorandums of understanding with the Kenya Space Agency in April 2025 and Angola's space programme office in June 2026; an MoU records intent, and no contract or budget has been announced under either. The NASA support announced in November 2025 as US$4 million is mostly staff time: the federal record shows US$179,529 committed to a research contract, and no award for the US$850,000 follow-on. The multi-satellite "virtual data centre" vision is future work, and results from the next mission, on TakeMe2Space's MOI-1a satellite, had not been published by 14 October. The article does not say how the US$8 million will be spent.

Gemini 4 Argon is live, and the cheapest way to use it in Kenya is KES 3,700 a month

Google's most capable model became publicly available on Tuesday 13 October in the Gemini app, AI Mode in Search, the Gemini API and Gemini for business; before that it had been limited to a group of cyber security teams. In Kenya the free tier (KES 0) and Google AI Plus (KES 900) do not include it, so the free year Google gives Kenyan university and college students, which is AI Plus, does not either. Google AI Pro at KES 3,700 does, marked as still rolling out, as do Ultra at KES 12,500 (five times Pro usage) and KES 37,000 (twenty times). Tech-ish puts KES 3,700 at about US$28.50, against US$19.99 for AI Pro in the United States. For developers, Kenya is on the supported-countries list, but Argon is on the paid API tier only, not the free tier or the Pro plan in AI Studio. Introductory prices of US$2 per million input tokens and US$10 per million output tokens last until 30 November, then double to US$4 and US$20 from 1 December. The context limit is 1,048,576 tokens, and Google's model card gives a knowledge cutoff of March 2026, so it needs web search for anything newer. A Pro subscriber may not see Argon in the model menu straight away.

MOBILITY

Bolt Plus costs KSh 280 a month, and a rider has to spend KSh 5,600 to earn that back

Bolt's new membership for Kenya costs KSh 280 a month or KSh 899 a year, with a free first month and then KSh 119 a month for the next six months. Members get 5% cashback on Bolt Basic rides and 10% on eligible Premium, Comfort and Business rides, paid into the Bolt Balance for future trips rather than taken off the fare, up to KSh 200 off delivery fees on qualifying orders, priority driver matching and three free ride cancellations a month. TechMoran's sums: at KSh 280 a Basic rider must spend KSh 5,600 a month to get the fee back, or KSh 2,800 on a 10% ride, and at the introductory KSh 119 those thresholds fall to KSh 2,380 and KSh 1,190. Uber One launched in Kenya in March 2025 at the same KSh 280 a month, or KSh 2,800 a year, so Bolt's annual plan works out to about KSh 75 a month. Bolt says the cost is carried by the company and its partners and does not cut driver, rider or courier earnings. The article gives no Kenyan subscriber numbers for either service and argues the harder problem is trust, citing past safety complaints and driver disputes with Bolt; that is the author's view.

FINTECH

Kuunda's US$5 million is a share sale, not new money for the company

Kuunda, a lending-as-a-service platform founded in 2018 that lets banks and fintechs offer overdrafts and working-capital loans through mobile money operators, payment aggregators and point-of-sale providers, announced US$5 million to expand into seven new markets. TechMoran's report describes it as a secondary sale, in which existing shareholders sell to other investors, so the money appears to go to the sellers rather than into the business, though the article does not say so outright. E4E Africa led, with existing backers Accion Ventures, Portugal Gateway Fund and Seedstars Africa Ventures; the valuation, the sellers and each investor's amount were not disclosed. Kuunda operates in Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, Mozambique and Malawi, and the seven new markets are not named. It raised US$7.5 million in October 2025. The company-reported figures are more than US$4.4 billion in lending activity, more than 870 million loans and 14.6 million agents, merchants and consumers reached, with no time period or independent check. Andrew Milne is named as chief executive, and the article does not say what the money will be used for.

Tesla finally shows the Roadster, nine years late, with thrusters and a 2028 production date

Tesla showed the second-generation Roadster on Thursday evening in Texas, early Friday morning in Nairobi, nearly nine years after it was first unveiled as a prototype in November 2017 with a promise of production in 2020. On stage the car drove up a ramp, briefly ran upside down, jumped a line of other Teslas and landed, using rear thrusters to slow its descent, and was clocked at 0.81 seconds from 0 to 60 mph; Elon Musk said Tesla expects to do better. Other numbers were claimed rather than shown: a presenter put the top speed at 235 mph or more, and Musk said it can go "much faster". Tesla's website lists three versions: Performance, with suction-based downforce and no thrusters, which the US$50,000 deposits taken since 2017 apply to; Thrust, with rear cold-gas thrusters, reservable with a US$250,000 downpayment; and an invite-only Rocket. No price, range or confirmed top speed was given, and Engadget's reporters estimate it will cost well over US$1 million. Musk said production comes in 2028, "for real", described it as "not necessarily a safety-optimized car", and said the Roadster will eventually be the only manually driven car Tesla makes. Whether a car like this can be legal on public roads is unclear. The next date to watch is Tesla's third-quarter results on Wednesday 21 October, with the call at 5:30 PM US Eastern time (12:30 AM Thursday in Nairobi), after third-quarter deliveries of 486,532 cars, against 497,099 a year earlier.
