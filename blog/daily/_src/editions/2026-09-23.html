Kenya has digitised 22,000 government services and still asks you to carry a birth certificate to the passport office — the ICT Secretary said so out loud at WSO2Con Africa in Nairobi.

Featured · Government

22,000 digital services, and a backend that is "still pretty manual"

ICT Secretary Mary N. Kerema put the gap plainly at WSO2Con Africa: the front doors are digital, the processing behind them is done by hand. The passport case is the one everyone recognises — applicants physically carry birth certificates and documents the government already holds, because no agency keeps an authoritative shared record. Kenya's answer is a Government Enterprise Architecture pushing cross-ministry integration over departmental silos, plus an interoperability platform now in development to allow controlled data-sharing between agencies. Kerema's framing was "one citizen, one journey, many government capabilities" — you ask for the service, not for the office. The AI ambition sits on top of that and not before it: once systems actually connect, agents can clear routine work and escalate the rest. Without the plumbing, there is nothing for them to act on.

SKILLS

KPC's Inuka fellowship placed 30% of its first cohort

Thirty fellows were picked from more than 1,000 applicants, half of them women, for a six-month in-person apprenticeship in Python, machine learning, dashboards, automation and data storytelling, run by Kenya Pipeline Company and its Foundation with Power Learn Project. Thirty percent have jobs — as fibre optic technicians, operational data analysts, business intelligence analysts and predictive analytics engineers — matched through PLP's Talent Hub. "The market informs the curriculum; students are trained, and thereafter they go for attachment and secure jobs," says KPC Foundation manager Rachel Gathoni. That ordering is the whole argument: build the course from the vacancies, not the other way round.

FINTECH

Equity moves payment approval to your face or thumb

Equity Mobile now authorises transactions with Face ID or fingerprint instead of a PIN, set up through Settings, Security, then confirmed once with the existing PIN — optional, so PIN users can carry on. The bank's three claims are worth separating: you cannot phish a fingerprint, which is true of the credential itself and not of the social engineering around it; the biometric stays on the handset and never reaches Equity's servers, which is the important architectural point; and a stolen phone is no longer enough to move money. Against Kenya's run of SIM-swap and social-engineering fraud, moving the second factor onto the device's secure element is the right direction.

INFRASTRUCTURE

Posta's parcels jumped 549% in a quarter — and still fell for the year

The Postal Corporation of Kenya handled 667,978 domestic parcels in the April–June quarter against 102,890 in the previous one, a 549.2% jump the CA attributes to a new partnership with an e-commerce consolidator and the lifting of restrictions on EMS items. Zoom out and the picture inverts: full-year parcel volumes fell 33.2% to 993,090 from 1.49 million, and domestic letters dropped 70.2%. Meanwhile private couriers took revenues up 6.7% to KSh6.70 billion on 14.4 million domestic parcels — roughly fifteen times what Posta moved. One good quarter on somebody else's e-commerce volume is not a turnaround.

STARTUPS

Bolt and the Red Cross will train 500 boda riders in first aid

The programme puts Kenya Red Cross first-aid training in front of 500 motorcycle riders on Bolt's platform — the people who reach a Nairobi crash site before an ambulance does, and who are themselves heavily represented in road casualty figures. It sits alongside Bolt's other Kenyan commitments this month, from the Comfort tier's expansion to its decade-in-Kenya numbers, but this one is cheap and unusually well targeted: riders are already first on scene, and until now almost none of them were trained for it.
