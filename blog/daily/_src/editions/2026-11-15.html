Kenya opens licensing for crypto firms: every exchange, wallet and broker now needs a CBK or CMA licence

In a joint notice dated 13 November, the Central Bank of Kenya and the Capital Markets Authority confirmed that the Virtual Asset Service Providers Regulations, 2026 are in force and said “any person(s) providing or intending to provide virtual asset services are required to apply for a licence”. The regulations put into operation the VASP Act, which took effect in November 2025; according to Prifinance's Dmitri Mihhailov, the Act's one-year transition window closed on 4 November. Each regulator licenses the services assigned to it in the Act's first schedule, publishes a licensing checklist on its website, and will publish a list of licensed providers; licences must also appear in the Kenya Gazette within 30 days. Penalties are steep. Companies face fines of up to KSh25 million. Individuals face up to KSh10 million, up to five years in prison, or both, and simply presenting yourself as running a crypto business in Kenya without a licence is an offence. Mihhailov warns that people who act as the local face of a platform are exposed even if they never touch client money. Kenyans moved about KSh740 billion in crypto in the year to June, according to Chainalysis, so this affects far more than a few start-ups. Why it matters to you: before you send money to an exchange or join a crypto project, check whether it appears on the CBK or CMA list once those are published.

What did Nvidia's security team know? The questions after Jensen Huang's exit

Nvidia's chief security officer of four years, David Reber, stepped down in August for health reasons and was replaced by Saša Zdjelar, formerly of ExxonMobil and Salesforce. Ten weeks later, Huang resigned amid scrutiny of his relationship with an executive at a Singapore data centre firm that US investigators suspect of moving Nvidia chips to China. Nothing public links the two changes, and Nvidia has not answered questions about what its security team knew. A board investigation and federal inquiries are under way.

Apple starts a 200,000-person study of how people use AI to answer health questions

The Apple Health AI Study app lets US iPhone users ask health questions and rate the answers, which draw on sources such as the New England Journal of Medicine through the company OpenEvidence. Participants can share Health app data such as sleep and heart rate. Apple says data is anonymised and will not be used to train AI models. It is US-only for now.

European investor Plural raises about US$1.1 billion for “moonshots”

The firm, an early backer of defence company Helsing and founded by tech operators including Wise co-founder Taavet Hinrikus, will put two-thirds of the €1 billion into a new early-stage fund and the rest into follow-on bets. Its focus is energy, AI infrastructure, defence and health. Big European funds backing deep tech is one more sign of where investor money is heading in 2026.
